# Founding Charter

Version 0.1

## 1. Purpose

New Jurisdiction is a public experiment in legal-system design, collaborative reasoning and machine-assisted legal work.

Its research question is:

> Can humans and AI build a legal system from first principles?

The experiment tests a process. It does not claim that an objectively perfect or universally correct legal system exists. Different rules advance different values, distribute costs differently and depend on contestable assumptions.

## 2. Hypothetical jurisdiction

New Jurisdiction is hypothetical. It has no territory, population or sovereign authority. Its texts do not create real legal rights, duties, remedies or institutions. Nothing in this repository is legal advice.

## 3. Accepted legal state

The accepted framework exists as version-controlled text on the repository's publication branch, normally `main`. A merged amendment changes the canonical experimental state. Branches and Pull Requests represent proposed alternatives until accepted under [GOVERNANCE.md](GOVERNANCE.md).

The repository must distinguish accepted law from questions, proposals, cases, evaluations and research. A model output, comment or open proposal is not law.

## 4. First-principles method

Proposals should state the assumptions, objectives and constraints from which they reason. They must not conceal normative choices as technical necessities or facts.

Material proposals should expose:

- the problem and intended objective;
- the proposed legal change;
- affected provisions, interests and dependencies;
- material trade-offs;
- the strongest argument against the change;
- expected failure modes and concrete test cases;
- sources or comparative material when used;
- unresolved uncertainty;
- authorship and material AI involvement.

## 5. Adversarial review

Criticism is part of the method. Reviewers are encouraged to identify contradictions, ambiguity, rights conflicts, institutional limits, perverse incentives and difficult cases—not merely improve drafting.

Disagreement must address the proposal and its reasoning. No contributor, model or maintainer is exempt from challenge.

## 6. Humans and AI systems

Humans may contribute directly or with AI assistance. AI systems may propose, analyze, critique and test legal rules.

AI output is not automatically authoritative. No model is an oracle, and no model or vendor receives privileged status. Differences between systems may become research evidence only when their provenance and limits are recorded accurately.

Human governance remains responsible for merge decisions and for the consequences of representing a change as accepted within the experiment.

## 7. Provenance and reproducibility

Important changes should preserve enough context to determine:

- what changed;
- why it changed;
- who or what proposed it;
- what human and AI involvement occurred;
- what objections and cases were considered;
- what sources were used;
- what decision was made.

Git history, Pull Requests and retained proposal records form part of that evidence. Provenance must not be invented where it was not captured.

## 8. Uncertainty and evidence

Contributors must distinguish, where relevant, facts, assumptions, inferences, normative choices and uncertainty. Citations must support the propositions for which they are offered. Synthetic cases test interpretation and consequences; they do not prove real-world effects.

Unknown information should remain identified as unknown. Deterministic validation checks form and references, not legal quality.

## 9. Evolution

The legal framework, taxonomy, contribution protocol and governance model may evolve through the same visible proposal and review process. Amendments to this Charter require explicit treatment as Charter amendments under [GOVERNANCE.md](GOVERNANCE.md).

The project must remain a serious, open experiment. It must not be converted into a claim of sovereignty, autonomous AI government, token voting, a DAO or a political manifesto without replacing this Charter through an expressly different project.
